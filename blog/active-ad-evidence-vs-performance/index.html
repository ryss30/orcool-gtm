What an active ad proves, and what to test next

By Orcool · October 21, 2026 · 8 min read

TL;DR

  • An ad listed as active gives you a dated observation of a competitor's creative. Its age does not reveal spend, acquisition cost or profit.
  • In Orcool's June 15, 2026 US keyword panel, 161 active ecommerce DTC ads had a median age of 14 days; 180 active subscription ads had a median age of 107 days. Those are ages at capture, not completed lifetimes or recommended test windows.
  • Use the reference to identify a message worth investigating. Record the source, check the proposed mechanism against your own customer evidence and product proof, then define the paid test that could support or reject it.

A competitor's ad has been active for months. Someone drops it into the creative brief with a familiar instruction: “This must be working. Let's do our version.”

Before a script is written, the team has made two assumptions: the ad performs well, and the visible hook explains that performance. The public record may support neither.

You can still use the reference. It shows a message another advertiser put into market, the proof they chose to show and the response they asked for. The useful decision is whether any of that deserves investigation for your brand. A source card makes that decision easier to review before the reference turns into a production commitment.

What our 1,061-ad snapshot actually measured

On June 15, 2026, Orcool collected a single US Meta Ad Library snapshot using a fixed panel of 36 keywords. It contained 1,061 active ads from 394 distinct advertisers.

Two buckets illustrate why the definition of the measurement matters:

Keyword bucketActive ads in the snapshotMedian days already live at capture
Ecommerce DTC16114
Subscription180107

The subscription ads visible that day were older at the median. That is the observation. The panel cannot tell us whether their economics were better, whether their messages were more persuasive or why advertisers kept them active.

It also cannot tell us how long a newly launched ad should last. An active ad has no observed end date in this snapshot. Ads that had already stopped are outside the sample. We therefore lack both the completed lifetimes and the full population of launches needed to estimate survival.

The buckets came from keyword searches and include competing advertisers and affiliates. They are not a census of each industry. These are historical June observations, not a fresh October market reading.

A 14-day median does not justify a day-14 DTC kill rule. A 107-day median does not justify funding a subscription test for 107 days. Both decisions need the advertiser's own economics and campaign evidence.

The aggregate was previously reported in our vertical breakdown. The narrower interpretation above governs its use here: days already live among active ads cannot establish performance or a completed lifespan.

For an individual reference, keep its direct source URL and native ad ID, observation date, recorded start date, market scope, visible message and format. Describe the message plainly enough that someone else can check it. “Shows how to pause a subscription” is inspectable. “Solves commitment anxiety” is an interpretation. “Converts hesitant buyers” requires outcome evidence.

That distinction should survive the handoff from researcher to creative director. Our evidence labels provide a fuller vocabulary, but the practical check is simple: could another person see this in the source, or have we supplied an explanation?

Worked example: should a subscription brand test a pause demonstration?

Illustrative example only. The brand, ad and customer inputs below are fictional teaching assumptions. They are not records from the June panel, an Orcool client case or measured results.

Imagine a subscription-box team reviewing a competitor ad that demonstrates pausing a delivery. The ad is listed as active. The team's question is whether to make its own pause demonstration.

The creative lead writes down the decision this way:

Decision inputFilled example
What the reference showsA customer pauses a delivery on screen before the ad asks viewers to subscribe.
What we inferShowing control over the next delivery may reduce hesitation about committing to a recurring purchase.
Our supporting customer evidenceFor this example, assume the team's own customer interviews contain questions about receiving boxes they do not need. Those interview records must be checked in a real project.
Our product proofAssume the brand genuinely allows customers to skip the next eligible delivery. The product owner must verify the steps, deadline and exceptions before the claim is used.
Proposed hypothesisShowing the skip action and its deadline before the purchase CTA will help hesitant new customers understand the commitment and improve first-order acquisition economics.
First executionA product demonstration using the brand's actual interface and approved terms.
Decision ownerThe creative lead owns the brief; the product owner verifies the claim; the advertiser's performance owner owns the test and result.

The competitor reference gets the question onto the table. The team's customer evidence and product proof determine whether it belongs in production. In a real project, check these against the current Brand Lens for the exact brand and market, including its audience, tone and claim limits.

Suppose the product check finds that skipping is only available after the second paid delivery. The proposed demonstration could create the wrong expectation. The team must revise or drop that promise before production, however old the competitor's ad is.

If the proof holds, the same hypothesis could support a product demonstration, a creator explanation or a static sequence. Choose the execution that can communicate the action and its conditions clearly. Changing format alone does not create a different hypothesis.

Write the review rule before launching

For this example, the performance owner selects cost per first paid order as the primary KPI and the current acquisition creative as the comparator. Before launch, that owner records the acceptable cost ceiling, comparable audience and offer conditions, attribution method, minimum usable delivery, review date and durability requirement. The June panel supplies none of those values.

The initial test changes the explanation of subscription control while holding the offer and purchase destination consistent. Claims and rights approval applies to the exact version launched.

At review, use the agreed evidence requirements:

  • Insufficient or incomparable delivery: record the test as inconclusive. Decide whether to fund a defined extension or stop under the advertiser's budget rules. Do not turn missing evidence into a creative verdict.
  • Usable delivery, KPI gate missed: record that this execution failed the agreed test. Inspect the result before deciding whether to change the demonstration or retire the hypothesis.
  • KPI gate met: continue the agreed durability check. A promising first reading becomes a winner only when the advertiser confirms that the result also holds under the specified conditions.

A higher click-through rate alone cannot satisfy a first-order acquisition-cost gate. If people click to inspect the terms but do not purchase, the team has learned something different from what the brief predicted.

Use this in your next creative review

Pick one competitor reference already in a brief. Ask its owner to add the direct source, capture date and one literal observation. Under that, write the proposed mechanism and the brand's own evidence for testing it. Finish with a named owner and one next action: verify the claim, collect the missing customer evidence or prepare a bounded test.

Review rule: if the only justification is “it has been running a long time,” return the reference for research. Production starts when the team can name the audience tension, show the product proof and state what paid result would change its decision.

This is the creative decision Orcool is built to support: carry market evidence into a defensible test, then preserve what that test taught. Concepts sit inside that process. Their value depends on the decision they enable and the learning that comes back from paid delivery.

The goal is a shorter Time-to-Winner, measured through the advertiser's KPI validation and paired with durability. A faster brief is useful progress. It cannot close that measurement by itself.

FAQ

Does an active ad prove that it performs well?

No. It establishes that the library listed the ad as active when observed. The June panel contains no spend, acquisition-cost, profit or campaign-role evidence. An ad's age cannot fill those gaps.

Is days already live the same as creative lifespan?

No. Days already live measures age at capture. This active-only snapshot has no completed end dates and excludes ads that had already stopped. It cannot estimate completed lifetimes or the survival rate of all launches.

Can I use the 14-day and 107-day medians to set test windows?

No. They describe two keyword buckets in one June 15, 2026 US snapshot. Set a test window from the advertiser's KPI, economics, usable delivery requirements and durability condition. The panel provides no recommended kill date or funding duration.

What should an active-ad reference change in a creative brief?

It should introduce a specific question about a visible message or mechanism. Keep the source and date attached, check that question against your own customer evidence and product proof, and define the paid test that could support or reject it. Winner status requires the advertiser's agreed KPI and durability checks.

Bring one decision question to the MCP

For a subscription brand in the US, start with: “Which current ads demonstrate pausing or skipping, what exactly do they show, and what evidence would we still need before testing that message for our brand?”

Ask for source-visible observations, separate inferences and missing inputs. That gives your team a research decision it can act on.
